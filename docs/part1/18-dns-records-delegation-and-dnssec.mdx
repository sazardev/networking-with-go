# DNS Records, Delegation, and DNSSEC

Chapter 1.9 introduced DNS as the internet's phone book: a name goes in, an address comes out. That is the right first mental model and a poor final one. DNS is a distributed database with delegation, caching, and a security model of its own. The phone book does not just map names to numbers; it decides who is allowed to answer for a name, how long the answer may be cached, and — if DNSSEC is in use — whether the answer is authentic.

This chapter is about that machinery. It is the layer where a single wrong record can take down email, a stale TTL can keep it down, and a missing signature can let an attacker answer in your place.

### Zones and Authority

DNS data is organized into **zones**. A zone is a portion of the namespace that one organization is responsible for — `example.com`, for instance, including everything below it unless a subdomain is delegated elsewhere. The servers that hold the authoritative copy of a zone are its **authoritative name servers**.

The **SOA** record at the top of every zone is the zone's metadata: the primary server, the responsible mailbox, a serial number, and timers for refresh, retry, expire, and negative caching. The serial number is how secondary servers know a zone changed. The timers are how caching resolvers know how long to remember a "no such name" answer.

Authority is delegated downward. The root zone does not contain a record for `www.example.com`; it contains a delegation to the `.com` servers. The `.com` zone contains a delegation to `example.com`'s name servers. Those servers hold the actual records.

```
Root (.)
  └── NS for .com  →  a.gtld-servers.net
        └── NS for example.com  →  ns1.example.com
              └── A  www.example.com  →  203.0.113.10
```

An **NS record** names a delegated server. When the delegated server's name is inside the zone it serves — `ns1.example.com` for `example.com` — the parent must also provide a **glue record**: the A/AAAA address of that server. Without glue, the resolver would need to resolve the name of the server it is trying to reach, which is a circular dependency.

<DeepDive title="Why delegation is what makes DNS scale">A single central database of every name would be impossible to keep current and impossible to serve at internet scale. Delegation pushes authority to the edge: the root only needs to know who runs `.com`, `.com` only needs to know who runs `example.com`, and `example.com` only needs to know its own hosts. Caching then removes most of the walking. A resolver can answer millions of queries while touching the root only rarely, because the answer to "who runs `.com`" is cached for as long as its TTL allows.</DeepDive>

### The Records That Matter

The common record types are not interchangeable, and a few rules trip people up constantly:

| Record | Purpose | Gotcha |
|---|---|---|
| `A` | Name to IPv4 address | Multiple A records load-balance |
| `AAAA` | Name to IPv6 address | Absence does not mean "no IPv6" |
| `CNAME` | Alias to another name | Cannot coexist with other records at the same name; not allowed at the zone apex |
| `MX` | Mail server, with a preference | Lower preference wins |
| `TXT` | Arbitrary text | Used for SPF, DKIM, DMARC, domain verification |
| `NS` | Delegation | Must point at a name, not an IP |
| `SRV` | Service location with port and priority | Used by SIP, XMPP, some service discovery |
| `CAA` | Which CAs may issue certificates | A missing CAA means any CA may |

The CNAME-at-apex rule deserves its own warning. A CNAME says "this entire name is an alias; look over there for everything." If `example.com` were a CNAME, it could not also have MX or NS records, because those would be shadowed. The usual workaround is a provider-specific ALIAS/ANAME record, which resolves at query time and returns an A/AAAA record instead of a CNAME.

<Warning title="A CNAME at the apex breaks email and delegation">Putting a CNAME on `example.com` instead of `www.example.com` is a classic outage. It conflicts with the MX records that route mail and the NS records that define the zone, and many providers will reject the zone outright. If a hosting provider tells you to point the root at them, use their ALIAS/ANAME feature, not a CNAME.</Warning>

### TTL, Caching, and Negative Answers

Every record carries a **TTL**. Resolvers may cache the answer for that many seconds. A long TTL reduces load and latency; a short TTL lets you change an address quickly. The trade-off is why migrations often start by lowering the TTL a day in advance: you cannot force caches to expire early, but you can make them expire sooner once they refresh.

Negative answers are cached too. The SOA's minimum TTL controls how long a resolver remembers "this name does not exist." That is why a newly created record can appear dead for a while even after you add it: resolvers are still holding the negative answer.

### DNSSEC: Signatures, Not Encryption

DNS was designed without authentication. An attacker who can answer before the real server — on a shared Wi-Fi, at a compromised resolver, or through cache poisoning — can return any address they like. **DNSSEC** adds cryptographic signatures to records so a validating resolver can tell whether an answer really came from the zone that owns the name.

The mechanism is a chain of trust:

- Each zone signs its records with a private key and publishes the signatures as **RRSIG** records.
- The public key is published as a **DNSKEY** record.
- The parent zone publishes a **DS** record containing a hash of that key.
- The root's key is the trust anchor, distributed out of band with resolvers.

A validating resolver walks the chain from the root down, checking each DS/DNSKEY pair. If a signature is missing or wrong, the answer is marked bogus and refused. If a zone is unsigned, the resolver can prove that too, using **NSEC/NSEC3** records that enumerate which names do not exist.

DNSSEC provides **integrity and authenticity**, not confidentiality. It does not encrypt queries; anyone on the path can still see which name you asked for. That is what **DNS over TLS** (port 853) and **DNS over HTTPS** (port 443) address, by encrypting the transport between the client and the resolver. They are complementary: DoT/DoH protect the conversation, DNSSEC protects the answer itself.

<DeepDive title="Why DNSSEC is not everywhere yet">DNSSEC is operationally demanding. Keys rotate, signatures expire, and a misconfiguration can make an entire domain unresolvable for validating resolvers. It also does not hide queries, so it solves only one threat. Adoption is highest at the root, the TLDs, and large providers; many ordinary domains still run unsigned. The result is a mixed internet where validation is useful but not guaranteed, which is why applications still rely on TLS certificates even when DNSSEC is present.</DeepDive>

<Axiom>DNS without DNSSEC tells you what someone said. DNSSEC tells you whether the zone that owns the name actually said it.</Axiom>

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does DNSSEC encrypt my DNS queries?**
No. It signs answers so they cannot be forged. To hide the query from the network, use DNS over TLS or DNS over HTTPS to a resolver you trust. The two mechanisms solve different problems.

**Why does a CNAME not work at the root of my domain?**
Because a CNAME claims the entire name as an alias, and the root must also carry NS and SOA records. Those cannot coexist with a CNAME. Use an ALIAS/ANAME record if your provider supports it.

**How long should a TTL be?**
Long enough to cut query volume and latency, short enough that a change propagates in a reasonable time. Five minutes to an hour is common for records that change; a day is common for stable infrastructure. Lower it before a planned migration.

**What is the difference between an authoritative server and a resolver?**
An authoritative server holds the real records for a zone and answers from them. A resolver asks other servers, caches the answers, and returns them to clients. Most clients talk to a resolver; the resolver talks to authoritative servers.

### Fun Facts and Notes

- The root zone is served by 13 named server identities, but each is anycast across many physical locations. There are far more than 13 machines.
- The `.com` zone alone contains well over a hundred million names, which is why TLD servers are built for query volume rather than for storing the whole internet.
- DNS uses UDP port 53 for most queries and TCP port 53 for large answers and zone transfers. DoT uses 853; DoH hides inside HTTPS on 443.
- The `SOA` serial number is often a date like `2026111701`, a convention that makes it obvious when the zone last changed.
